Power & institution

With a boss / authority

The nervous system does not distinguish cleanly between a frightening parent and a frightening boss. Authority activates the oldest relational software we have — and the behaviors that kept us safe at eight years old reappear in the performance review.

What makes this context different

Power differentials compress the nervous system. When approval, livelihood, or status is controlled by another person, the window of tolerance narrows. Behaviors that would be readable as defense in a peer relationship become invisible — they look like professionalism, ambition, or work ethic.

Bradshaw’s work on the toxic shame of the institutional self is central here: many adults spend their entire careers re-enacting a childhood relationship with a critical or withholding parent, never recognizing the repetition.

Key lens for this context

Bradshaw on shame and authority. Porges on the neuroception of hierarchy. Van der Kolk on hypervigilance in power-differential environments. Saxby on the inner marriage — how we seek authority outside when we’ve lost the internal one.

Patterns that show up here
Fawning / people-pleasing
Move toward Control
The logic: If I anticipate what they need and deliver it before being asked, I won’t be rejected, criticized, or fired.
The cost: Chronic self-betrayal. No one knows what you actually think. Resentment accumulates.
Resistance: Disagreeing with authority still feels physically dangerous, even when it isn’t.
What it protects: The childhood wound of conditional approval — love that required performance.
A way through: One honest sentence per week that represents your actual view. Start small. Notice you survive it.
Shrinking / self-erasure
Move away Numb
The logic: The smaller I make myself, the less of a target I am.
The cost: Invisibility. Good work goes unrecognized. Capacity goes undeveloped.
Resistance: Being seen means being evaluated — and possibly found lacking.
What it protects: The terror of being exposed as not enough, which was the original wound.
A way through: Claim one piece of work as yours. Out loud. To the person who has power.
Covert rebellion
Control Discharge
The logic: I can’t say no directly, so I say yes and then don’t.
The cost: Trust erodes. Reputation suffers. The relationship becomes adversarial without ever having the conversation.
Resistance: Direct refusal still activates the fear response of the child who was punished for saying no.
What it protects: Autonomy — the only way I knew how to have it.
A way through: Practice saying “I can’t do that, but here’s what I can do.” Direct, boundaried, and survivable.
Idealization → devaluation
Move toward Discharge
The logic: I attach to a powerful other to feel safe — until they inevitably disappoint, and then the same force that idealized them destroys them.
The cost: A succession of mentors, bosses, and institutions that start as salvation and end as the enemy.
Resistance: Maintaining ambivalence — seeing someone as both good and flawed — requires tolerating the anxiety of not knowing where you stand.
What it protects: The grief of the original authority figure who could not be both powerful and safe.
A way through: Hold the complexity. This person is limited. That doesn’t make them the enemy.
