Non-human & transitional

With animals

For many heavily armored people, animals are the first safe attachment. No judgment, no agenda, no script. The regulation that feels impossible with humans becomes available here — and what happens in that space is worth paying close attention to.

What makes this context different

Animals activate the ventral vagal system — Porges’s social engagement circuitry — without triggering the threat-appraisal mechanisms that human relationships engage. The animal doesn’t evaluate. It responds to state, not narrative.

This makes the animal relationship a window into what regulation actually feels like in the body. Many people discover in their relationship with an animal what they have been unable to access with other humans — and this becomes a bridge, not a destination.

Key lens for this context

Porges on the ventral vagal system and neuroception of safety. Van der Kolk on animal-assisted therapy and body-level regulation. The animal as a “transitional object” in the Winnicottian sense — a bridge between the defended interior and the relational world.

What this relationship reveals
The animal as the only safe attachment
Move toward
What it shows: The capacity for attachment is intact. The body knows how to connect. The defenses are specific to human relationships, not to intimacy itself.
The risk: The animal becomes a substitute rather than a bridge — and the avoidance of human intimacy is organized around their presence.
The question worth sitting with: What is available here — in this body, in this moment of contact — that I haven’t allowed myself with a person?
As a bridge: Use what you know about how to be with an animal as a map for what you want to practice with a human. Slow down. Follow their cues. Be in the room.
Over-caretaking the animal
Control Move toward
What it shows: The same caretaking strategy that appears in human relationships — managing their needs as a way of feeling needed and safe.
The cost: The animal’s actual needs get managed rather than met. The relationship becomes about the caretaker’s regulation, not the animal’s wellbeing.
The question: What am I meeting in myself through their care?
A way through: Practice being with the animal without doing anything. Just present.
Grief at the animal’s loss as proxy grief
Discharge
What it shows: When the grief of an animal’s death exceeds what the relationship itself might account for, it is often carrying older, unlived grief.
What it opens: The body finally gets to grieve — for the animal, and for everything else that was never mourned. This is not pathological. It is an opening.
A way through: Let the grief be as large as it is. Don’t apologize for it or minimize it. Follow it where it goes.
