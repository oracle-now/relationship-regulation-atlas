Why do we do what we do—and what does it cost us?

The Relationship Regulation Atlas maps the strategies people use to manage overwhelming emotion, closeness, and conflict. Every behavior here is an attempt to survive something. This atlas names the logic, the cost, and the way through.

Three ways to use this

For yourself

Browse the matrix and notice which behaviors feel uncomfortably familiar. The resistance column is where the real work starts.

With a partner

Use the Cycle Map together. Each person picks their move. The loop it shows you is not an accusation—it's a description of something you're both caught in.

In therapy

The atlas was built with the clinical frameworks of Orna Guralnik, the Gottmans, Van der Kolk, Dick Schwartz, and John Bradshaw. It is designed to complement, not replace, therapeutic work.

Standing on their shoulders

This atlas grew out of the people whose work helped me put language around things I was already noticing. Some helped me understand the body. Some helped me think more clearly about attachment. Some helped me hold desire, shame, fear, and protection in the same frame without flattening any of it. I did not include these voices just because they are respected. I included them because I actually followed them over time. I read them, watched them, learned from them, and found their ideas quietly shaping how I think about relationships.

Core lineage

Orna Guralnik

I pulled Orna in because she helped me see that the real action in a relationship is often happening underneath the obvious conversation. Watching her work made me pay more attention to pauses, misattunement, defensiveness, and the moment right before someone pulls away.

Bessel van der Kolk

I included Bessel because he gave me a stronger language for shutdown, flooding, numbness, and the body's memory. He helped me keep the atlas grounded in the fact that people are not only reacting from thoughts.

Stephen Porges

Porges helped me think about behavior through nervous system state. That gave me a way to look at silence, anger, clinginess, or collapse with more curiosity and less judgment.

John Bradshaw

I pulled Bradshaw in because his work keeps bringing you back to the younger self under the adult reaction. He helped me remember that a lot of what looks dramatic, avoidant, controlling, or needy is also old hurt trying to keep itself safe.

Elliot Saxby

I included Elliot because his work helps me think about inner division—the part that performs, the part that hides, the part that wants love, and the part that does not trust it. That tension is all over this atlas.

Richard Schwartz

Schwartz gave me one of the deepest emotional permissions in this whole project. Every part is trying to help. That idea softened the writing and changed the whole tone of the map.

Sue Johnson

Sue Johnson helped me think in cycles. I kept coming back to the way her work makes room for both people's fear at once, without losing sight of impact or responsibility.

John & Julie Gottman

I included the Gottmans because their work gives people concrete handles. It is one thing to say a relationship is struggling. It is another thing to recognize criticism, contempt, defensiveness, and stonewalling while they are actually happening.

Esther Perel

I pulled Esther in because she gave language to tensions that are easy to feel and hard to explain—especially the tension between love and desire. Her work helped me keep erotic life in the atlas without making it flat or moralistic.

Julie Menanno

I included Julie because I remember how accessible her attachment language felt right away. There was something about the way she translated complicated dynamics into daily relationship moments that made the whole subject feel less abstract.

Linda Thai

Linda belongs here in a more personal way. I was in her somatic certification classes, and what I learned there helped me feel regulation as something physical and relational, not just conceptual. I still think about overwhelm, overthinking, flooding, and the way the body needs resourcing, co-regulation, and a wider window of tolerance.

Living conversation

These are some of the more current voices that also shaped the feel of the atlas. I think of them less as references and more as people whose language, nuance, or emotional honesty kept sharpening my own.

@thelovingavoidant

Posts that made room for avoidant attachment without turning it into a free pass. That balance helped me write the distancing rows with accountability and care at the same time.

@jillianturecki

The idea I kept getting was that painful patterns repeat for a reason—not because people are weak, but because some part of them still believes the pattern is solving something. That logic is built right into the resistance column.

@drlaurenfogelmersy

I learned that desire problems inside a relationship need care, language, and honesty, not instant blame. That helped me write the erotic rows with more nuance, especially where sex, distance, shame, and longing get mixed together.

@alan_robarge_psychotherapist

What I kept getting from Alan's work was that attachment wounds do not stay in childhood. They keep showing up in adult behavior, especially in the push for closeness and the fear of it. That logic is part of the spine of the atlas.

@heymisstherapist

Posts that made simple relational truths feel sharp again—especially around listening without fixing and the difference between solving a problem and helping someone feel understood. That helped me think more carefully about repair.

@jessica.rozak

Jessica belongs here because she spoke directly to people who live in their heads. There is a very specific pain in understanding yourself well and still feeling stuck. That helped shape the intellectualizing row in a more honest way.

@healwithrachel

What I learned from Rachel's work is that attachment pain is not just a story you tell about childhood. It is something that lives in the body and shows up in the present. That kept the nervous system side of the atlas feeling concrete.

@jordanpickellcounselling

With Jordan, I remember a tone more than one single idea. Clear, steady, trauma-informed, never performative. That kind of tone influenced how I wanted this atlas to feel when talking about hard patterns.

@innerworktherapy

I remember learning that knowing what you need and actually asking for it are often two very different things. That helped me think more carefully about insecure attachment, shame, and the fear people carry around needing anything from anyone.

These are not just references to me. They are part of the emotional and intellectual lineage of the atlas. Some gave me backbone. Some gave me texture. Some helped me hold people with more complexity and more care.
